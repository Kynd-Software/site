# Decision Fatigue and ADHD

For most people, making a small decision — what to have for lunch, which route to take, whether to reply now or later — barely registers as effortful. For someone with ADHD, these micro-decisions can accumulate into something overwhelming. By mid-afternoon, the ability to choose anything at all can simply shut down.

## What decision fatigue is

Decision fatigue is the deterioration of decision-making quality after a long session of making choices. It's a well-documented phenomenon in psychology — everyone experiences it to some degree. But in ADHD, it hits earlier, harder, and more frequently.

This is because every decision relies on executive functions:

- **Working memory** — holding the options in mind while evaluating them
- **Prioritisation** — determining what matters most
- **Impulse inhibition** — not just grabbing the first option
- **Future thinking** — considering consequences of each choice
- **Initiation** — actually committing to and acting on the decision

When these systems are already working harder than usual, they deplete faster.

## Why ADHD makes it worse

### Every decision costs more

For a neurotypical brain, many decisions are semi-automatic — handled by well-worn routines and heuristics. For an ADHD brain, fewer decisions reach that automatic stage. Each choice requires active, effortful processing. The tax on every decision is higher, so the budget runs out sooner.

### Difficulty with prioritisation

"Which of these things should I do first?" is a question that requires ranking — and ranking requires a clear sense of what matters most. ADHD brains often struggle to differentiate between priorities. Everything feels equally urgent (or equally unimportant), making even simple sequencing decisions exhausting.

### Perfectionism as compensation

Many people with ADHD develop perfectionism as a coping mechanism — if I can't trust my impulses, I'll overthink everything to avoid mistakes. This turns minor decisions into major deliberations. Choosing a font, writing a message, picking a restaurant — each becomes a research project.

### The paralysis response

When decision fatigue reaches its peak, the result isn't a bad decision — it's no decision at all. Complete paralysis. Standing in the kitchen unable to choose what to eat. Staring at a to-do list unable to pick a starting point. Opening and closing apps without doing anything.

This isn't laziness. It's a system that has run out of the resources needed to make choices.

## How it shows up

**The morning overwhelm.** Getting ready involves dozens of micro-decisions: what to wear, what to eat, what order to do things in, what to bring. For many people with ADHD, mornings are exhausting before they've technically started their day.

**The open browser tab problem.** Researching a purchase and ending up with forty tabs, unable to commit to any option. The more options, the more paralysed.

**Meal planning collapse.** Deciding what to eat three times a day, every day, indefinitely is a particular form of torture for ADHD brains. The question "what do you want for dinner?" can feel genuinely distressing.

**Work prioritisation freeze.** Multiple tasks, no clear hierarchy, limited time. The brain stalls trying to determine where to start.

**Avoiding decisions by default.** Letting subscriptions renew, staying in unsatisfying situations, going with whatever someone else chooses — not from genuine indifference, but from decision exhaustion.

## Working with it

**Reduce the number of decisions.** Capsule wardrobes. Meal rotations. Default routines. Every decision you can eliminate or pre-make is one fewer drain on the system.

**Use "good enough."** Deliberately practice choosing the first acceptable option rather than the optimal one. For low-stakes decisions, "fine" is fine.

**Time-box decisions.** "I'll spend five minutes choosing, then I'm going with whatever I've got." External time pressure can break paralysis.

**Decide the night before.** When possible, make tomorrow's decisions tonight — when the specific time pressure isn't present and you can think more freely.

**Outsource where comfortable.** Let someone else choose the restaurant. Use a meal delivery service. Set up automatic payments. There's no prize for making every decision yourself.

**Recognise the paralysis.** When you notice you've been standing still for several minutes, unable to start — that's decision fatigue talking. The answer isn't "try harder to choose." It's often "do anything at all" or "rest and come back."

**Front-load important decisions.** If you know decision fatigue worsens through the day, put the important choices early. Schedule creative work and major decisions for your peak hours.

## What it looks like from outside

From the outside, decision fatigue in ADHD can look like:

- Indecisiveness or "not knowing what you want"
- Avoidance or procrastination
- Going along with others' choices too readily
- Analysis paralysis on things that "shouldn't be hard"
- Shutting down or withdrawing when asked to choose

If you're supporting someone with ADHD, offering a limited choice ("A or B?") is significantly more helpful than an open question ("What would you like?"). And sometimes "I'll decide for us today" is the kindest thing you can say.
