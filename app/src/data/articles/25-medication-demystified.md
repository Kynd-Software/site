# Medication Demystified

ADHD medication is one of the most effective treatments in all of psychiatry — and one of the most misunderstood. The stigma, the myths, and the fear surrounding it prevent many people from accessing something that could genuinely transform their daily functioning.

This article isn't advocacy for medication (it's a personal choice, and not right for everyone). It's information — clear, factual, and free from the moral panic that usually surrounds the topic.

## What ADHD medication actually does

ADHD medication doesn't sedate. It doesn't create artificial focus. It doesn't change your personality. It doesn't give you abilities you don't have.

What it does: it adjusts neurotransmitter availability in the prefrontal cortex — primarily dopamine and norepinephrine — allowing the brain's executive function system to work more effectively.

Think of it this way: if ADHD means the brain's management system is under-resourced, medication provides the resources. You still have to do the managing — but now you have the tools to do it.

## The two main categories

### Stimulant medications

These are the first-line treatment for ADHD. Despite the name, they don't "stimulate" in the way caffeine or energy drinks do. They increase dopamine and norepinephrine availability in the prefrontal cortex.

**Methylphenidate-based:**
- Ritalin, Concerta, Equasym, Medikinet, Xaggitin
- Available in immediate-release (3–4 hours) and extended-release (8–12 hours) formulations

**Amphetamine-based:**
- Elvanse/Vyvanse (lisdexamfetamine) — a prodrug that's converted to dexamphetamine in the body
- Dexamfetamine (Amfexa)
- Available in immediate-release and extended-release formulations

**How they work:** They increase the availability of dopamine and norepinephrine in the synapse — either by blocking reuptake (methylphenidate) or by promoting release (amphetamines).

**Onset:** Effects typically begin within 30–60 minutes.

**Response rate:** Approximately 70–80% of people respond to one or both classes of stimulant.

### Non-stimulant medications

Used when stimulants aren't appropriate, aren't tolerated, or aren't sufficient alone.

**Atomoxetine (Strattera):**
- A selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor
- Takes 4–6 weeks to reach full effect
- Provides 24-hour coverage
- No abuse potential
- Particularly helpful for anxiety comorbidity

**Guanfacine (Intuniv):**
- An alpha-2 adrenergic agonist
- Helps with emotional regulation, impulsivity, and hyperarousal
- Often used alongside stimulants
- May help with Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria
- Takes 2–4 weeks for full effect

**Clonidine:**
- Similar to guanfacine
- Often used for sleep difficulties alongside ADHD
- Can help with tic disorders that co-occur with ADHD

## What medication feels like

This is the question everyone asks and the one that's hardest to answer universally. Common descriptions from people who respond well to medication:

- "Like putting on glasses for the first time — I didn't know this was how thinking could feel"
- "The mental noise quietens. I can hear my own thoughts one at a time"
- "I can choose what to pay attention to, rather than being pulled by whatever's loudest"
- "I still have ADHD. But I can work *with* my brain instead of fighting it"
- "The wall between me and starting things gets shorter"
- "Emotions still happen — but I have a moment to choose how to respond"
- "I'm still me. I'm just me with a functioning prefrontal cortex"

It doesn't feel euphoric (at the right dose). It doesn't feel like a drug. It feels like removal of an obstacle.

## What medication doesn't do

**It doesn't fix everything.** Medication improves executive function capacity, but it doesn't build skills, repair relationships, undo years of shame, or organise your filing system. It provides the *ability* to do these things — the doing still requires effort, habits, and often therapy or coaching alongside.

**It doesn't work 100% of the time.** Efficacy varies with sleep, food, hormones, stress, and general health. Some days are better than others, even with medication.

**It doesn't eliminate ADHD.** It reduces symptoms to a more manageable level — typically by 50–75%. You'll still have ADHD. You'll just have more resources to manage it.

**It doesn't last 24 hours (usually).** Most formulations wear off. You'll likely experience differences between medicated and unmedicated periods of the day.

## Common concerns addressed

### "Isn't it basically speed?"

Amphetamine-based medications are chemically related to illicit amphetamines, but the comparison is misleading. The difference is in:
- **Dose** — therapeutic doses are far lower
- **Delivery** — extended-release formulations provide steady, gradual delivery rather than a sudden spike
- **Context** — in a brain with dopamine deficiency, the medication normalises rather than elevates function

At therapeutic doses in people with ADHD, stimulant medication doesn't produce a "high." It produces normal.

### "Will I become addicted?"

Research consistently shows that:
- Therapeutic use of ADHD medication does not lead to addiction
- People with ADHD who take medication as prescribed have *lower* rates of substance abuse than those who are untreated
- Extended-release formulations have minimal abuse potential
- The distinction between physical dependence (needing a consistent dose for function) and addiction (compulsive use despite harm) is important

### "Will it change my personality?"

At the right dose: no. You should feel more like yourself, not less. If medication makes you feel flat, robotic, or unlike yourself, the dose or medication type needs adjusting.

Some people worry about losing their creativity. Research doesn't support this fear — in fact, many people find they can *execute* creative ideas more effectively with medication, because the gap between having ideas and acting on them shrinks.

### "What about side effects?"

Common initial side effects (many reduce after the first few weeks):
- Reduced appetite (particularly around midday)
- Difficulty sleeping (if medication is taken too late)
- Slight increase in heart rate
- Dry mouth
- Mild headaches

These are generally manageable and often dose-related. More significant side effects (mood changes, chest pain, tics) should always be discussed with your prescriber immediately.

### "What about long-term effects?"

Long-term studies spanning decades show:
- No significant adverse effects on brain development in children
- No increase in cardiovascular events at therapeutic doses
- Protective effects against substance abuse, accidents, and academic failure
- The main long-term risk is a slight reduction in expected height in children (1–2cm), which often normalises after treatment

The long-term risks of *untreated* ADHD (accidents, substance abuse, relationship breakdown, career underachievement, mental health crises) significantly outweigh medication risks in most cases.

## Finding the right medication

Medication is not one-size-fits-all. Finding the right option involves:

**Titration:** Starting at a low dose and gradually increasing until the optimal balance of benefit and side effects is found. This typically takes 4–8 weeks.

**Trying different options:** If one medication doesn't work or causes side effects, others may be better. Not responding to methylphenidate doesn't mean medication won't work — it means you need to try amphetamine-based options (or vice versa).

**Timing adjustments:** When you take medication, how long it lasts, and whether you need coverage at different times of day — all of these are adjustable.

**Combining approaches:** Some people benefit from a long-acting medication for daytime coverage plus a short-acting booster for evenings, or a stimulant combined with a non-stimulant.

**Regular review:** Needs change over time. What works now may need adjusting in a year. Regular prescriber check-ins ensure medication remains optimally effective.

## Medication is a tool, not a cure

The most effective ADHD management typically combines:
- Medication (improving brain function capacity)
- Education (understanding your neurology)
- Strategies and systems (external scaffolding)
- Therapy (addressing shame, building skills, processing history)
- Environmental design (creating conditions that support your brain)

Medication makes all the other pieces more accessible — but it doesn't replace them.

## The choice is yours

Nobody should be pressured into medication. Nobody should be shamed out of it. It's a medical treatment for a neurological condition, and the decision to use it or not is personal, informed, and revisable.

If you're considering it: speak to a qualified prescriber, ask questions, start carefully, and give it a fair trial. If it works, it can be genuinely life-changing. If it doesn't, or you prefer not to, there are other paths forward.
