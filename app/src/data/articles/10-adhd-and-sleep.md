# ADHD and Sleep

"I can't switch my brain off." It's one of the most common complaints from people with ADHD at bedtime. The relationship between ADHD and sleep is bidirectional, deeply entangled, and affects nearly every aspect of daily functioning. Yet it remains one of the least discussed aspects of the condition.

## The scale of the problem

Research consistently shows that 50–75% of adults with ADHD experience significant sleep difficulties. This isn't a coincidence or a side effect — it appears to be intrinsic to how ADHD brains regulate arousal and circadian rhythm.

Common sleep difficulties in ADHD include:

- Difficulty falling asleep (sleep onset insomnia)
- Difficulty waking up (sleep inertia)
- Delayed sleep phase — the internal clock running 1–3 hours behind societal norms
- Restless, non-restorative sleep
- Frequent waking
- Difficulty maintaining a consistent sleep schedule

## Why ADHD disrupts sleep

### The racing mind

The ADHD brain doesn't have a reliable "off switch." When external stimulation drops — as it does at bedtime — the mind often fills the gap with its own content. Thoughts race, spiral, or loop. Problems get replayed. Tomorrow gets rehearsed. Creative ideas arrive at midnight with the urgency of a revelation.

This isn't anxiety (though anxiety can coexist). It's a brain that struggles to regulate its own activity level in the absence of external input.

### Circadian rhythm differences

Research by Dr Sandra Kooij and others has found that many people with ADHD have a delayed circadian rhythm — their natural sleep-wake cycle runs later than the typical 11pm–7am window. Melatonin onset (the hormone that signals sleepiness) occurs later, body temperature patterns are shifted, and the natural point of alertness peaks later in the day.

This means the person with ADHD who "can't sleep before 1am" may not be undisciplined — their biological clock may genuinely be set differently.

### Arousal regulation

ADHD involves differences in the brain's arousal regulation systems. The transition from wakefulness to sleep requires a gradual reduction in arousal — a dimming process. In ADHD, this dimming is often unreliable. The brain either stays at full alertness or crashes from exhaustion, with little graceful transition between.

### Stimulation-seeking at bedtime

For a dopamine-hungry brain, bedtime is boring. The temptation to scroll, read, watch, or do "one more thing" is powerful — not from lack of discipline, but because the brain is seeking the stimulation it needs to feel settled. Paradoxically, some people with ADHD need low-level stimulation (audiobooks, background TV, white noise) to quiet the mind enough to sleep.

## The vicious cycle

Poor sleep worsens ADHD symptoms. Worsened ADHD symptoms make sleep harder. The cycle feeds itself:

1. Poor sleep → reduced executive function → more impulsivity, worse attention, higher emotional reactivity
2. Worse ADHD symptoms → more difficulty winding down → later bedtime → less sleep
3. Repeat

This means that addressing sleep is often one of the highest-leverage interventions for ADHD — yet it's frequently treated as secondary to attention and focus symptoms.

## Sleep and ADHD medication

Stimulant medications have a complex relationship with sleep:

- **They can help** — by improving self-regulation and reducing the racing mind, some people actually sleep better on medication
- **They can hinder** — if taken too late in the day, stimulants can delay sleep onset
- **Timing matters** — working with a prescriber to optimise medication timing is often essential
- **Rebound effects** — as medication wears off, some people experience a rebound of symptoms (including mental restlessness) that can affect sleep

Non-stimulant medications and melatonin supplementation are sometimes used specifically to address sleep difficulties in ADHD.

## Morning: the other half of the problem

ADHD sleep difficulties aren't just about falling asleep — they're about waking up. Sleep inertia (the groggy, disoriented state upon waking) is often severe and prolonged in ADHD. The transition from sleep to wakefulness relies on the same arousal regulation systems that ADHD affects.

Common morning experiences:

- Multiple alarms with no memory of turning them off
- Extreme grogginess lasting 30–60+ minutes
- Difficulty with the "wake up and do things" sequence that others handle automatically
- Being labelled as "lazy" for something that's physiologically driven

## Working with ADHD sleep

**Consistency over perfection.** A "good enough" routine done consistently helps more than a perfect routine attempted sporadically.

**Honour your chronotype where possible.** If your natural rhythm runs late, and your life allows any flexibility, work with it rather than against it. A consistent 1am–9am schedule may be healthier than a forced 11pm–7am pattern that results in lying awake for two hours every night.

**Wind-down protocols.** The brain needs transitional time. Dimming lights, reducing stimulation, and following the same sequence of steps each night gives the brain predictable cues.

**Strategic boredom.** Some people find that deliberately boring content (monotonous podcasts, dull audiobooks, familiar TV shows) gives the brain just enough stimulation to quiet the racing thoughts without creating engagement.

**Manage the environment.** Blackout curtains, cool temperatures, weighted blankets (the deep pressure can help with restlessness), white noise or brown noise.

**Bright light in the morning.** Light exposure within 30 minutes of waking helps reset the circadian clock. Light therapy lamps or morning walks can genuinely shift sleep timing.

**Be honest with your clinician.** Sleep difficulties are a treatable aspect of ADHD, not an unavoidable consequence. Medication timing adjustments, melatonin, and other interventions can help significantly.
