# The Three Presentations of ADHD

When people picture ADHD, they often imagine a child bouncing off walls. That image captures one version — but only one. ADHD presents in different ways, and understanding this is key to recognising it in yourself or the people around you.

## The current framework

The DSM-5 (the diagnostic manual used by clinicians) describes three presentations of ADHD:

### 1. Predominantly Inattentive

Sometimes still informally called "ADD," this presentation is characterised by:

- Difficulty sustaining attention on tasks that aren't inherently stimulating
- Frequently losing things — keys, phone, train of thought
- Appearing not to listen when spoken to directly
- Struggling to follow through on instructions or finish tasks
- Avoiding tasks that require sustained mental effort
- Being easily distracted by unrelated thoughts or stimuli
- Forgetting daily activities and appointments

This is the quiet presentation. People with predominantly inattentive ADHD often fly under the radar. They're not disruptive — they're daydreaming, drifting, or silently overwhelmed. This is especially common in women and girls, and is a major reason for late diagnosis.

### 2. Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive

This is the presentation most people recognise:

- Fidgeting, tapping, squirming
- Difficulty staying seated when expected to
- Feeling restless or "driven by a motor"
- Talking excessively
- Blurting out answers before questions are finished
- Difficulty waiting for a turn
- Interrupting or intruding on others

In adults, the physical hyperactivity often becomes internalised — experienced as mental restlessness, racing thoughts, or a constant need for stimulation rather than literal running around.

### 3. Combined Presentation

This is the most commonly diagnosed presentation, where someone meets criteria for both inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive symptoms. It's not simply "both at once" — the balance between the two can shift over time and across different environments.

## Why the labels only tell part of the story

These categories are useful for diagnosis, but they have significant limitations:

**Presentations change over time.** A child diagnosed with the combined presentation may present as predominantly inattentive by adulthood, as hyperactive symptoms often become less visible with age. The underlying neurology hasn't changed — the expression has.

**Context matters enormously.** Someone might appear perfectly focused in a one-to-one conversation but completely unable to sustain attention in a group meeting. They might be highly organised at work (where external structure exists) but chaotic at home (where they have to generate their own). ADHD doesn't look the same in every setting.

**The criteria were built on a narrow sample.** The diagnostic criteria were largely developed by studying young boys. This means that presentations more common in women, girls, and adults — particularly inattentive presentations — were historically overlooked. The field is catching up, but slowly.

**Symptoms don't capture the full experience.** The diagnostic criteria focus on observable behaviours. They don't adequately capture the internal experience — the emotional dysregulation, the time blindness, the shame, the exhaustion of compensating. These are often the aspects that affect quality of life most.

## Beyond the checklist

If you're trying to understand ADHD — whether in yourself or someone else — the three presentations are a starting point, not the whole picture. They tell you roughly where someone's challenges cluster. They don't tell you what it feels like, how it interacts with their personality, or what support will actually help.

The most useful question isn't "which type do I have?" It's "where do I struggle, and what would help?"
