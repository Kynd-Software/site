# ADHD Across the Lifespan

ADHD doesn't arrive at age six and leave at eighteen. It's a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition that changes shape as the demands of life change around it. Understanding how it manifests at different stages helps explain why so many people are diagnosed late — and why the support that worked at one age may not work at another.

## Childhood

This is where ADHD is most commonly recognised — partly because schools create an environment that makes executive function differences highly visible.

**What it often looks like:**
- Difficulty sitting still, waiting turns, or following multi-step instructions
- Losing belongings repeatedly
- Inconsistent academic performance — brilliant one day, struggling the next
- Social difficulties — interrupting, missing social cues, intense reactions
- Being labelled as "disruptive," "away with the fairies," or "not reaching potential"

**What's often missed:**
- The quiet inattentive child who isn't causing problems but is silently lost
- The child who compensates through intelligence or people-pleasing
- Emotional meltdowns attributed to "behavioural problems" rather than dysregulation
- Giftedness masking ADHD (and vice versa)

Children with ADHD aren't choosing to misbehave. They're struggling with a system that assumes a level of self-regulation their neurology doesn't yet support.

## Adolescence

Puberty adds complexity. Executive function demands increase sharply — more homework, more social navigation, more expectation of independence — while the prefrontal cortex (already developing differently in ADHD) won't fully mature until the mid-twenties.

**What it often looks like:**
- Academic performance dropping as structure decreases and workload increases
- Risky behaviour — impulsivity combined with adolescent novelty-seeking
- Social struggles intensifying — rejection sensitivity, difficulty maintaining friendships
- Sleep problems worsening (ADHD frequently disrupts circadian rhythm)
- Low self-esteem building from years of negative feedback

**What's often missed:**
- Girls internalising their struggles as anxiety or depression
- The teenager who works three times as hard as peers for similar results
- Substance use as unconscious self-medication
- The shift from external hyperactivity to internal restlessness

Many people who were "fine" in primary school hit a wall in secondary school. The scaffolding that parents and teachers provided falls away, and suddenly the executive function gaps are exposed.

## Early adulthood

University, first jobs, independent living. The external structures of school disappear almost entirely. For many with undiagnosed ADHD, this is where things unravel — or where compensatory strategies become unsustainably expensive in terms of energy.

**What it often looks like:**
- Difficulty managing finances, paperwork, appointments
- Struggles with time management and deadlines without external accountability
- Job-hopping or underperformance despite capability
- Relationship difficulties — forgetting things, appearing unreliable
- Burnout from years of overcompensating

**What's often missed:**
- The high-achieving person whose success comes at enormous hidden cost
- Anxiety and depression that are actually downstream of unmanaged ADHD
- The person who thrives in crisis but can't handle routine

This is the stage where many people first seek help — often presenting with anxiety, depression, or burnout. The ADHD underneath is frequently the last thing identified.

## Middle adulthood

Life complexity peaks. Career responsibilities, perhaps children (possibly children with ADHD, prompting recognition of the same patterns in yourself), aging parents, household management. The executive function demands are relentless.

**What it often looks like:**
- Chronic overwhelm and the sense of "drowning in admin"
- Difficulty maintaining household routines without enormous effort
- Career plateaus despite talent — promotion requires exactly the organisational skills that are hardest
- Relationship strain from years of forgotten tasks and emotional reactivity
- Seeking diagnosis after recognising ADHD in their child

**What's often missed:**
- The parent (often the mother) holding everything together through sheer force of will
- The professional who has built elaborate systems to mask their difficulties
- The cumulative emotional toll of decades without understanding

## Later adulthood

ADHD in older adults is almost entirely unresearched. What we do know:

- Some cognitive changes of aging can interact with ADHD in complex ways
- Loss of work structure through retirement can be destabilising
- Medication considerations change with age and other health conditions
- A lifetime of undiagnosed ADHD leaves psychological scars — grief, anger, "what if?"

For those diagnosed late in life, there's often a profound mix of relief ("it wasn't my fault") and loss ("I could have had help decades ago").

## The thread that runs through

Across all life stages, the core remains consistent: executive function works differently. What changes is the environment's demands and the individual's capacity to compensate. When demands outstrip compensation, that's when ADHD becomes most visible — and most distressing.

Understanding this trajectory matters because it reframes ADHD not as a childhood condition you outgrow, but as a lifelong difference that requires lifelong understanding and, often, lifelong support.
