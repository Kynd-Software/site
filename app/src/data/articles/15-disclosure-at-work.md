# Disclosure at Work: When, How, and Whether To

Telling your employer about your ADHD is one of the most personal decisions you'll face in your working life. There's no universally right answer — it depends on your workplace culture, your role, your relationship with your manager, and what you actually need. This article isn't here to tell you what to do. It's here to help you think it through.

## The legal landscape (UK)

In the UK, ADHD is considered a disability under the Equality Act 2010 if it has a substantial and long-term adverse effect on your ability to carry out normal day-to-day activities. This means:

- You're protected from discrimination related to your ADHD
- Your employer has a legal duty to make "reasonable adjustments"
- You cannot be treated less favourably because of your ADHD
- You don't have to disclose unless you want to — the protection exists regardless

However, your employer can only make adjustments they know about. If you don't disclose, they can't be expected to accommodate needs they're unaware of.

*Note: Legal frameworks differ by country. If you're outside the UK, check your local disability discrimination legislation.*

## Reasons people choose to disclose

**Access to adjustments.** Flexible working hours, written instructions, noise-cancelling headphones, quiet workspace, adjusted deadlines — these become easier to request with a formal disclosure.

**Explaining patterns.** If you're frequently late, inconsistent, or struggle with certain tasks, disclosure provides context that prevents misinterpretation as laziness or incompetence.

**Reducing the energy of masking.** Hiding ADHD takes enormous effort. Disclosure can reduce the constant performance of neurotypicality.

**Building authentic relationships.** Being open about how your brain works allows colleagues to understand and support you more effectively.

**Protecting yourself.** If performance issues arise later, having a documented disclosure means reasonable adjustments should have been in place — which matters if things escalate.

**Normalisation.** Every person who discloses makes it slightly easier for the next person. Visibility matters.

## Reasons people choose not to disclose

**Stigma is real.** Despite legal protection, unconscious bias exists. Some managers still associate ADHD with unreliability or incompetence. This is wrong, but it's real.

**It can't be un-said.** Once disclosed, you can't take it back. If the workplace culture shifts or a new manager arrives with different attitudes, the information is already out there.

**It's not always relevant.** If your ADHD is well-managed and you don't need adjustments, there may be no practical reason to disclose.

**Career concerns.** Some people worry about being passed over for promotion, excluded from projects, or seen as less capable. In good workplaces, this shouldn't happen. But not all workplaces are good.

**It's deeply personal.** You are never obligated to share medical or neurological information with your employer. Privacy is a valid choice.

## Questions to ask yourself

Before deciding, consider:

1. **Do I need adjustments I can't get without disclosing?** If you need formal accommodations, disclosure is usually necessary.

2. **What's the workplace culture like around neurodiversity?** Is there visible acceptance? Employee resource groups? Or silence and stigma?

3. **Who would I be telling?** Your direct manager? HR? An occupational health team? Each carries different implications.

4. **What do I want to achieve?** Be clear about your goal — adjustments, understanding, protection, or simply authenticity.

5. **What's the worst realistic outcome?** Not the catastrophic fear, but the realistic risk. Can you manage that?

6. **Do I have support if it goes badly?** Union representation, HR processes, or external advocacy if needed.

## How to disclose (if you choose to)

### Start with what you need, not a medical history

You don't need to explain ADHD comprehensively. Focus on:
- What you need (specific adjustments)
- Why it helps (brief explanation)
- What you already do well (reframe the conversation)

**Example:** "I have ADHD, which means I work best with written instructions and flexible deadlines where possible. I do my strongest work when I can vary my environment. I'd like to discuss a few adjustments that would help me perform at my best."

### Choose your audience carefully

- **Direct manager** — if you have a good relationship and need day-to-day adjustments
- **HR** — if you want formal documentation without involving your manager initially
- **Occupational health** — if available, they can recommend adjustments without disclosing your specific condition to your manager (just the accommodations needed)

### Put it in writing

A verbal conversation is fine for the initial discussion, but follow up in writing. This creates a record of what was discussed and agreed — protection for both sides.

### You control the depth

You can say "I have ADHD" without explaining every symptom, your full history, or your medication. You're disclosing a condition, not performing a confession. Share what's relevant to the adjustments you need. The rest is yours.

## Reasonable adjustments to consider requesting

- Flexible start/end times (particularly if you have a delayed sleep phase)
- Written agendas before meetings
- Written follow-up of verbal instructions
- Permission to use noise-cancelling headphones
- A quieter workspace or ability to work from home
- Adjusted deadlines with clear intermediate milestones
- Regular check-ins rather than annual reviews
- Permission to move, stand, or fidget during meetings
- Task management support or coaching
- Modified communication preferences (email over verbal, or vice versa)

These are not special treatment. They're adjustments that allow equal access to doing good work.

## If it goes well

Many people find that disclosing leads to better relationships, less stress, and more effective working. A good employer responds with curiosity and support. The conversation might even prompt others to share their own experiences.

## If it goes badly

If you experience negative treatment following disclosure:
- Document everything — dates, conversations, changes in behaviour
- Raise it with HR formally
- Contact your union if you have one
- Seek advice from ACAS (UK) or equivalent advisory service
- Know that you have legal protection and options

A negative response says everything about the workplace and nothing about you.
