# ADHD and the Brain

"Just try harder" is perhaps the most damaging phrase someone with ADHD can hear. It assumes the problem is effort. It isn't. The problem is that the brain's regulatory systems work differently — and no amount of willpower can override neurochemistry.

Understanding the neuroscience behind ADHD doesn't require a medical degree. It just requires letting go of the idea that ADHD is about character, and accepting that it's about biology.

## The prefrontal cortex

The prefrontal cortex sits behind your forehead and is responsible for executive functions — the brain's project manager. It handles:

- Planning and prioritising
- Impulse control
- Working memory (holding information while you use it)
- Emotional regulation
- Shifting attention between tasks
- Evaluating consequences before acting

In ADHD, the prefrontal cortex develops more slowly (roughly 2–3 years behind in children) and functions differently across the lifespan. It's not damaged — it's wired differently. This means the "management system" of the brain operates with different constraints.

## Dopamine: the motivation molecule

Dopamine is a neurotransmitter involved in motivation, reward, pleasure, and attention. It's often simplified as the "feel-good chemical," but its primary role is actually signalling that something is worth paying attention to or worth pursuing.

In ADHD brains, dopamine signalling is different:

- **Lower baseline dopamine activity** in key brain regions, particularly the prefrontal cortex
- **Faster dopamine reuptake** — meaning dopamine is cleared from the synapse more quickly, reducing its effect
- **Altered dopamine receptor density** in some areas

This is why:

- Tasks that are novel, urgent, or personally interesting can generate enough dopamine to enable intense focus (hyperfocus)
- Tasks that are routine, delayed in reward, or externally imposed feel almost physically impossible to engage with
- The gap between "knowing what to do" and "being able to do it" is so wide

It's not that people with ADHD lack motivation. It's that their brain's motivation system requires different conditions to activate.

## Norepinephrine: the alertness system

Norepinephrine (noradrenaline) works alongside dopamine in attention and arousal regulation. It helps the brain determine what to focus on and maintain alertness.

In ADHD, norepinephrine signalling is also altered, contributing to:

- Difficulty sustaining attention
- Inconsistent alertness and energy levels
- Problems with working memory
- The "fog" that many people with ADHD describe

This is why some ADHD medications (particularly non-stimulants like atomoxetine) target the norepinephrine system specifically.

## Brain structure differences

Neuroimaging research has revealed consistent structural differences in ADHD brains:

- **Slightly smaller total brain volume** in childhood (this normalises with age in most cases)
- **Thinner cortex** in prefrontal regions during development
- **Differences in the basal ganglia** — structures involved in reward processing, habit formation, and motor control
- **Altered connectivity** between brain regions, particularly in the default mode network (the network active during daydreaming and mind-wandering)

These are statistical differences across groups. No brain scan can diagnose ADHD in an individual — but these findings confirm that ADHD has a clear neurological basis.

## The default mode network

One of the more interesting findings in ADHD research concerns the default mode network (DMN). This is the brain network that activates when you're not focused on a specific task — during daydreaming, mind-wandering, and self-referential thinking.

In neurotypical brains, the DMN quiets down when a task demands focus. In ADHD brains, the DMN often doesn't fully suppress during tasks — it keeps intruding. This helps explain why people with ADHD experience:

- Intrusive thoughts during focused work
- Difficulty "switching off" internal chatter
- A tendency to drift to more interesting mental content
- The feeling that the brain has "a mind of its own"

## Why "just try harder" doesn't work

When someone says "just focus" or "just try harder," they're essentially asking someone to override their neurochemistry through force of will. This is like asking someone with poor eyesight to "just see better."

People with ADHD are often trying extraordinarily hard — harder than the people telling them to try harder. The effort simply doesn't produce the expected result because the underlying systems process effort and reward differently.

This isn't an excuse. It's an explanation. And it points toward solutions that actually work — environmental design, medication, external systems, and support — rather than solutions that rely on the very system that's working differently.

## What this means in practice

Understanding the neuroscience changes the conversation from "what's wrong with you?" to "what does your brain need?" Some practical implications:

- **Medication makes sense** — stimulant medications increase dopamine and norepinephrine availability in the prefrontal cortex, helping it function more effectively. They don't sedate or change personality — they give the brain access to its own regulatory system.
- **Environment matters more** — if internal regulation is less reliable, external structure becomes essential, not optional.
- **Interest-based motivation is real** — working with the dopamine system (novelty, urgency, personal meaning, challenge) rather than against it isn't laziness. It's neuroscience-informed strategy.
- **Inconsistency is the hallmark** — the brain's regulatory systems are variable, which is why performance is inconsistent. Good days don't disprove the condition; they demonstrate what happens when neurochemistry cooperates.
