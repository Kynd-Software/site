# Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria

Imagine receiving a slightly curt text message and spending the next three hours convinced the person hates you. Imagine a piece of constructive feedback landing like a physical blow. Imagine avoiding applying for jobs, asking someone out, or sharing creative work — not because you don't want to, but because the possibility of rejection feels unbearable.

This is Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD), and for many people with ADHD, it's one of the most painful aspects of the condition.

## What RSD is

Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria describes an extreme emotional response to perceived or actual rejection, criticism, or failure. The term was coined by Dr William Dodson, a psychiatrist specialising in ADHD.

Key word: *perceived*. RSD can be triggered not just by real rejection, but by the possibility of rejection, the interpretation of neutral signals as rejection, or even imagined scenarios of future rejection.

The "dysphoria" part is important — it's not simply feeling sad about rejection (which everyone experiences). It's an intense, often sudden, overwhelming emotional pain that can feel physical. People describe it as:

- "Like being punched in the chest"
- "An immediate, total collapse"
- "Everything goes dark and nothing else exists"
- "White-hot shame flooding my entire body"

These reactions are involuntary, often disproportionate to the trigger, and typically short-lived (minutes to hours) but extraordinarily intense while they last.

## How it connects to ADHD

RSD isn't a separate condition — it appears to be intrinsic to ADHD, stemming from the same neurological differences:

### Emotional dysregulation

ADHD brains have less effective top-down emotional control. When a rejection signal is perceived, the emotional brain (amygdala) fires intensely, and the prefrontal cortex doesn't modulate the response effectively. The emotion arrives at full strength without the usual buffering.

### Pattern recognition and history

Most people with ADHD have a lifetime of accumulated rejection experiences — being told off more frequently as children, receiving more negative feedback, experiencing more social difficulties, failing to meet expectations despite trying. The brain becomes primed to detect rejection because historically, rejection has been frequent.

### Speed of processing

The emotional response happens faster than the rational processing. By the time the thinking brain catches up with "that text probably wasn't meant rudely," the emotional brain has already flooded the system with pain.

### Dopamine and reward sensitivity

Altered dopamine signalling in ADHD may mean that social reward (acceptance, praise) is experienced more intensely — which means its withdrawal (rejection, criticism) is also felt more acutely.

## How RSD manifests

RSD doesn't always look the same. Common patterns include:

### The inward collapse

Sudden, overwhelming sadness or shame. Withdrawal. Silence. The person disappears into themselves, convinced they're worthless, unlovable, or fundamentally broken. This can look like depression from the outside but typically passes faster.

### The outward flash

Intense anger or defensiveness. A heated response to mild criticism. Snapping at the person perceived as rejecting. This is the fight response to emotional pain — and it often damages relationships, creating a cycle of more rejection.

### The avoidance pattern

Not applying, not asking, not trying, not sharing. If you never put yourself forward, you can't be rejected. RSD drives chronic under-achievement in people who avoid any situation where rejection is possible — which is most opportunities.

### People-pleasing

If rejection is unbearable, preventing it becomes paramount. RSD can drive extreme people-pleasing, overwork, saying yes to everything, and bending yourself to meet every perceived expectation — at enormous personal cost.

## What triggers it

- Direct criticism (even constructive)
- A perceived change in someone's tone or behaviour
- Not receiving an expected response (no reply to a message)
- Feeling excluded or overlooked
- Making a mistake publicly
- Comparison to others
- Ambiguous social signals interpreted negatively
- Anticipating future rejection
- Memories of past rejection

The trigger doesn't need to be objectively significant. A raised eyebrow can be enough.

## The impact on life

RSD affects:

**Career** — avoiding promotion opportunities, not advocating for yourself, leaving jobs after perceived slights, difficulty with performance reviews.

**Relationships** — interpreting neutral behaviour as rejection, pushing people away preemptively, difficulty with conflict, needing constant reassurance.

**Creativity** — not sharing work, not starting projects that might be judged, abandoning things rather than risking criticism.

**Mental health** — the cumulative weight of frequent intense emotional pain contributes to anxiety, depression, and low self-worth.

**Identity** — when you've been in pain this frequently for this long, it becomes easy to believe you're fundamentally flawed rather than neurologically different.

## What helps

### Recognise the pattern

Naming RSD — understanding that this specific, intense emotional response has a neurological basis — is powerful. It creates a tiny gap between the trigger and the spiral: "This is RSD. This feeling is real but the interpretation may not be."

### Wait before acting

RSD peaks fast and passes (relatively) fast. If you can delay acting on the feeling — not responding to the email for an hour, not quitting the job today, not ending the relationship this minute — the intensity often reduces enough for rational thought to re-enter.

### Reality-test with trusted people

"I received this message and I feel like they hate me. Can you read it and tell me what you see?" Having someone who knows about your RSD and can offer a neutral reading is invaluable.

### Medication

Dr Dodson notes that certain medications can reduce RSD intensity:
- Alpha-2 agonists (guanfacine, clonidine) — may reduce the intensity of the emotional response
- Stimulant medication — improves overall emotional regulation
- Some people find that RSD specifically improves with medication even when other ADHD symptoms require additional support

### Self-compassion practices

The pain of RSD is real. You don't need to justify or minimise it. But you also don't have to believe every story it tells. The feeling is valid; the conclusion ("they hate me," "I'm worthless") usually isn't.

### Build a rejection-resilience practice

Not exposure therapy (overwhelming yourself with rejection isn't helpful), but gradually expanding your tolerance. Small asks. Low-stakes sharing. Building evidence that rejection, when it does come, is survivable.

## For the people around them

If someone you care about has RSD:

- Your reassurance matters more than you might think
- A slightly cold response from you can cause genuine pain — not because they're manipulating you, but because their nervous system is wired this way
- "I'm not upset with you, I'm just tired" is a sentence worth saying
- Don't dismiss the pain. Don't say "you're overreacting." The reaction is proportionate to what they're feeling, even if it's not proportionate to the trigger
