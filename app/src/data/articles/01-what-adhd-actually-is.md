# What ADHD Actually Is (And Isn't)

Most people have heard of ADHD. Far fewer understand what it actually means to live with it. The name itself — Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder — is arguably misleading. It's not so much a deficit of attention as a difference in how attention is regulated.

## The basics

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition. That means it's present from early brain development — it's not caused by bad parenting, too much screen time, or a lack of willpower. It affects roughly 5–7% of children and around 2.5–4% of adults worldwide, though many researchers believe the adult figure is underestimated due to decades of under-diagnosis.

At its core, ADHD is a condition of executive function. Executive functions are the brain's management system — they handle planning, prioritising, starting tasks, sustaining effort, managing emotions, and holding information in working memory. When these functions work differently, the downstream effects touch nearly every part of daily life.

## What it isn't

**It isn't laziness.** People with ADHD often work significantly harder than their peers to achieve the same output — they're just doing it with a system that doesn't support them in the way most environments assume.

**It isn't a lack of intelligence.** ADHD has no correlation with IQ. Many people with ADHD are highly capable, creative thinkers who struggle not with understanding but with execution.

**It isn't something you grow out of.** While some hyperactive symptoms may become less visible with age, the condition persists into adulthood in the majority of cases. The challenges simply shift — from running around the classroom to missing deadlines, losing track of conversations, or struggling with emotional regulation.

**It isn't just about focus.** ADHD affects time perception, emotional responses, motivation, memory, and impulse control. Attention is just the most visible tip.

## The executive function picture

Dr Russell Barkley, one of the leading researchers in the field, describes ADHD as primarily a disorder of self-regulation rather than attention. His model identifies several key executive functions affected:

- **Inhibition** — the ability to pause before acting
- **Working memory** — holding information in mind while using it
- **Emotional self-regulation** — managing the intensity of emotional responses
- **Self-motivation** — generating drive without immediate external reward
- **Planning and problem-solving** — organising steps toward a goal

When these systems work differently, the result isn't a character flaw. It's a brain operating with a different set of constraints.

## The dopamine piece

ADHD brains appear to have differences in dopamine signalling — particularly in the prefrontal cortex, the area responsible for executive function. Dopamine is involved in motivation, reward, and attention regulation. This is why tasks that are novel, urgent, or personally interesting can command intense focus (hyperfocus), while routine or low-stimulation tasks feel almost physically impossible to start.

This isn't a choice. It's neurochemistry.

## Why it matters

Understanding ADHD accurately matters because misunderstanding leads to shame. Decades of being told to "just try harder" or "stop being so disorganised" leave marks. When people understand that their struggles have a neurological basis — and that effective support exists — it changes everything.

ADHD isn't a label that limits. It's an explanation that frees.
