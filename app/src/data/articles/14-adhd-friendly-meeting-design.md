# ADHD-Friendly Meeting Design

Meetings are where many ADHD brains go to suffer quietly. The format — sitting still, listening passively, tracking multiple speakers, remembering what was said, and contributing at appropriate moments — relies heavily on exactly the executive functions that ADHD affects. But it doesn't have to be this way.

Small design changes can make meetings more accessible for neurodivergent participants — and, as it happens, more effective for everyone.

## Why meetings are hard for ADHD brains

### Sustained passive attention

Listening to someone talk requires sustained attention with minimal external stimulation. For ADHD brains, this is the hardest form of attention. Without something to anchor focus (visual input, physical engagement, personal relevance), the mind drifts within minutes.

### Working memory load

Following a discussion requires holding previous points in mind while processing new ones. When working memory is limited, earlier points get overwritten by newer ones. By the time it's your turn to speak, you may have lost track of the thread entirely.

### Impulse management

Having a thought and needing to hold it until an appropriate moment is exhausting. The thought feels urgent — if you don't say it now, it'll disappear. But interrupting is socially penalised. The result is either blurting (and being seen as rude) or the thought evaporating (and being seen as disengaged).

### Transition difficulty

Context-switching into a meeting (stopping whatever you were focused on) and out of a meeting (returning to productive work) both carry high executive function costs. Back-to-back meetings are particularly punishing because there's no recovery time between transitions.

### Time estimation

Knowing how long you've been talking, whether the meeting is running over, and when to wrap up a point — all require time perception that ADHD affects.

## Design principles

### 1. Share the agenda in advance

What: Send a clear agenda before the meeting — ideally 24 hours ahead, minimum 1 hour.

Why: This allows ADHD participants to prepare their thoughts when they have capacity, rather than needing to generate responses in real-time. It also provides a cognitive anchor — something to reference when attention drifts.

### 2. Keep it short

What: Default to 25 minutes instead of 30, or 50 instead of 60. Question whether the meeting needs to exist at all.

Why: Attention fades over time for everyone, but for ADHD brains, the drop-off is steeper. Shorter meetings with focused outcomes respect everyone's cognitive limits.

### 3. Use visual anchors

What: Shared screen with the agenda, notes being taken in real-time, visual frameworks, whiteboards.

Why: Visual input provides something for the eyes to anchor on, supports working memory (you can see what was already discussed), and offers an alternative processing channel for auditory information.

### 4. Allow movement

What: Explicitly normalise fidgeting, standing, doodling, walking during calls. For in-person meetings, provide fidget objects or allow people to stand.

Why: For many people with ADHD, physical movement actually improves focus — it provides the low-level sensory input that keeps the brain's arousal level in the optimal zone for attention.

### 5. Build in interaction

What: Don't let any one person talk for more than 5–7 minutes without interaction. Use polls, quick reactions, round-robins, or breakout pairs.

Why: Passive listening is the hardest attention mode. Active participation — even brief — resets the attention clock and re-engages the brain.

### 6. Provide written follow-up

What: Send written notes, action items, and decisions after the meeting.

Why: If something was missed due to attention drift, it's recoverable. If working memory lost a point, it's documented. This removes the pressure of needing to catch everything in real-time.

### 7. Assign clear action items with owners and deadlines

What: End every meeting with explicit "who is doing what by when."

Why: Vague outcomes ("let's circle back on this") rely on initiative and memory — both ADHD challenges. Specific, assigned, deadlined actions give the executive function system something concrete to work with.

### 8. Respect transition time

What: Don't schedule meetings back-to-back. Build in 10–15 minute buffers.

Why: The transition cost between meetings is real. Without buffer time, ADHD participants arrive at the next meeting still mentally processing the previous one, starting at a deficit.

### 9. Make cameras optional

What: For virtual meetings, don't require cameras unless genuinely necessary.

Why: Managing your own facial expressions and physical presentation while trying to focus is an additional cognitive load. Some people focus better when they can move freely without being watched.

### 10. Start with a brief orientation

What: Begin with 30 seconds of "here's what we're covering today and what we need from you."

Why: This gives the brain a framework to organise incoming information around. Without it, the first few minutes are spent figuring out what the meeting is even about.

## For individuals in meetings

If you have ADHD and struggle with meetings, these personal strategies can help:

- **Take notes by hand** — the physical act anchors attention more than passive listening
- **Doodle** — it looks like you're not paying attention, but for many ADHD brains, doodling actually improves auditory processing
- **Use a fidget under the table** — meet the need for movement discreetly
- **Write down thoughts immediately** — don't try to hold them for the "right moment"
- **Position yourself where you can see the speaker** — visual anchoring helps
- **Request the agenda in advance** — it's a reasonable accommodation

## The broader benefit

Every change listed above makes meetings better for neurotypical participants too. Shorter meetings, clear agendas, visual aids, written follow-ups, interaction — these are just good meeting design. ADHD-friendly design is human-friendly design.

When we design for the people who struggle most, we raise the floor for everyone.
