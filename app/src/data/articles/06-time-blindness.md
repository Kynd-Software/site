# Time Blindness: Living Without an Internal Clock

Ask someone with ADHD what time it is, and they'll often be wrong by hours. Not minutes — hours. This isn't carelessness or not caring about being punctual. It's a genuine neurological difference in how time is perceived and processed.

## What time blindness actually means

Time blindness is the informal term for a real phenomenon: impaired time perception and time management in ADHD. Research by Dr Russell Barkley identifies it as one of the core executive function deficits, arising from differences in the brain's internal timing mechanisms.

For someone with time blindness, time doesn't flow in a predictable, felt way. It's more like:

- **Time is either "now" or "not now."** There's no intuitive sense of "soon" or "in a bit." Something is either happening right now or it exists in an undifferentiated future.
- **Duration is hard to estimate.** A task that took 20 minutes feels like it took 5. A conversation that lasted an hour feels like it lasted 15 minutes. Or the reverse — sometimes a short wait feels endless.
- **Time passes without being noticed.** People describe "looking up" and realising hours have gone — not in a relaxed, leisurely way, but with genuine shock.
- **Deadlines don't feel real until they're immediate.** A deadline next week might as well be a deadline next year. It only becomes real in the final hours.

## The "now or not now" brain

Dr Barkley describes the ADHD experience of time as essentially two zones:

1. **Now** — whatever is happening in this moment
2. **Not now** — everything else, whether it's five minutes or five months away

This is why someone with ADHD might be perfectly aware that they have a meeting at 2pm, yet somehow arrive at 2:20. They knew about the meeting. They intended to be on time. But "2pm" remained in the "not now" zone until it was suddenly, catastrophically, "now" — and by then it was too late.

## How it shows up in daily life

**Chronic lateness** — not from rudeness, but from genuinely misjudging how long things take. Getting ready takes longer than the brain estimates. The drive takes longer. The "one quick thing" before leaving absorbs twenty minutes without being noticed.

**Missed deadlines** — the work is important, the intention is sincere, but the deadline doesn't create urgency until it's almost passed. Then everything happens in a panicked rush.

**Difficulty planning** — planning requires imagining a future sequence of events and estimating how long each will take. Both of these rely on time perception.

**Hyperfocus time distortion** — when deeply engaged in something interesting, time can vanish entirely. Three hours pass in what feels like twenty minutes.

**Difficulty with "boring" waits** — conversely, unstructured waiting (queues, waiting rooms, loading screens) can feel interminable. Five minutes without stimulation feels like thirty.

## Why it isn't a character flaw

People with time blindness aren't disrespectful of others' time. They aren't selfish. They often feel intense guilt and shame about their lateness and missed commitments. Many overcompensate — arriving extremely early for important events because they can't trust their own sense of time, then sitting anxiously waiting.

The underlying issue is neurological. The brain structures involved in time perception — including the cerebellum, basal ganglia, and prefrontal cortex — function differently in ADHD. This is measurable in laboratory settings. People with ADHD consistently show impaired performance on time estimation and time reproduction tasks.

## Working with it, not against it

Since you can't simply develop an internal clock through willpower, the approach is to create external time awareness:

**Make time visible.** Analogue clocks where you can see time's passage. Timer apps that show time draining. Visual countdowns.

**Use transition alarms.** Not just "meeting at 2pm" but "start getting ready at 1:30" and "leave the house at 1:45." Multiple waypoints, not just the destination.

**Time your routines.** Actually measure how long your morning routine takes. Most people with ADHD significantly underestimate this. Use the real number, not the wished-for number.

**Build in buffers.** If you think something will take 30 minutes, schedule 45. If you think you need to leave at 1:45, set your "leave" alarm for 1:35.

**Body doubling for transitions.** Having someone else involved in a transition ("we're leaving in 10 minutes") creates external time pressure that the brain can register.

**Forgive the past.** Every late arrival, every missed deadline, every forgotten appointment — these weren't moral failings. They were a brain operating without the equipment that others take for granted.
