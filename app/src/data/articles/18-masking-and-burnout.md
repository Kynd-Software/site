# Masking and Burnout

There's a version of you that shows up to work, remembers social norms, maintains eye contact at the right moments, arrives on time, keeps your desk tidy, responds to emails within 24 hours, and never blurts out the wrong thing.

That version is exhausting to maintain. And the cost of maintaining it — day after day, year after year — is burnout.

## What masking is

Masking (sometimes called camouflaging) is the conscious or unconscious suppression of neurodivergent traits in order to appear neurotypical. It's performing the version of yourself that the world expects, rather than the version that comes naturally.

For people with ADHD, masking might look like:

- Forcing yourself to sit still when every part of you needs to move
- Suppressing thoughts and impulses rather than expressing them
- Over-preparing for social interactions to avoid "mistakes"
- Maintaining meticulous organisation through enormous unseen effort
- Hiding emotional responses behind a neutral face
- Pretending to remember things you've forgotten
- Arriving exactly on time through intense anxiety management rather than natural time sense
- Performing interest in conversations where your attention has drifted

From the outside, a well-masked person looks fine — perhaps a bit reserved or anxious, but functional. From the inside, it's like wearing a costume that's three sizes too small.

## Why people mask

### Survival

Many people learn to mask in childhood — not as a conscious choice, but as an adaptive response to repeated negative feedback. When being yourself results in punishment, criticism, social rejection, or confused looks from adults, you learn to perform a more acceptable version.

### Social acceptance

Neurotypical social norms are the default. Deviating from them carries social costs — being seen as rude, weird, intense, flaky, or "too much." Masking is the price of belonging.

### Professional survival

Work environments often have narrow expectations of behaviour. The employee who fidgets, blurts, forgets meetings, and needs things repeated is less likely to be promoted — regardless of their actual capability.

### Internalised ableism

After years of being told your natural way of being is wrong, many people genuinely believe their unmasked self is unacceptable. Masking isn't just about others' perceptions — it's about your own belief that the real you isn't good enough.

## The cost of masking

Masking is not free. It uses cognitive resources — executive function, working memory, self-monitoring — that are already limited in ADHD. The result is depletion.

### Cognitive exhaustion

Every masked interaction costs energy. By the end of a workday, there may be nothing left for home life, personal projects, or basic self-care. Partners see the unmasked version — tired, irritable, depleted — while colleagues see the performative version and assume the person is "fine."

### Identity confusion

When you've masked for long enough, it becomes difficult to know which reactions are "you" and which are performance. "Do I actually enjoy this, or have I trained myself to appear to?" This identity erosion is psychologically damaging.

### Delayed diagnosis

Successful masking means the outside world doesn't see the struggle. This means teachers, parents, doctors, and employers don't flag concerns. Many late-diagnosed adults masked so effectively that no one believed them when they finally sought assessment.

### Emotional suppression

Masking emotions — particularly the intensity of ADHD emotions — means they don't disappear. They accumulate. They emerge later, often in safe spaces (home, alone in the car, in bed at night) or in disproportionate eruptions over minor triggers.

### Physical symptoms

Chronic masking activates stress responses. Over time, this contributes to:
- Muscle tension (particularly jaw, shoulders, stomach)
- Headaches and migraines
- Digestive issues
- Fatigue that sleep doesn't resolve
- Immune system suppression

## ADHD burnout

ADHD burnout is what happens when masking and compensation can no longer be sustained. It's distinct from (though related to) workplace burnout:

**Characteristics of ADHD burnout:**
- Executive functions deteriorate further — things that were hard become impossible
- Strategies that previously worked stop working
- Emotional regulation collapses
- Physical exhaustion that isn't resolved by rest
- Withdrawal from social life and activities
- Inability to maintain even basic routines
- Intense shame about "failing" at things you previously managed
- Feeling of complete breakdown — "I used to be able to do this and now I can't"

### What triggers it

- Life transitions that increase demands (new job, parenthood, bereavement)
- Accumulation of sustained masking without adequate rest
- Loss of support systems
- Hormonal changes (perimenopause is a common trigger in women)
- Attempting to maintain performance after diagnosis without actually changing anything
- Simply running out of time — the debt catches up

### Why it's misdiagnosed

ADHD burnout is frequently misdiagnosed as:
- Depression (the symptoms overlap significantly)
- Chronic fatigue syndrome
- Anxiety disorders
- Personality changes
- "Not coping" (with the implication that trying harder would help)

The difference: ADHD burnout is caused by the unsustainable over-use of executive functions. Recovery requires reducing demands, not just treating mood.

## Recovery

### Reduce demands

This isn't optional self-care advice — it's medical necessity. Something in the system needs to give. This might mean:
- Reducing working hours
- Temporarily lowering standards (good enough, not perfect)
- Asking for help with things you've always managed alone
- Saying no to commitments
- Letting some balls drop deliberately rather than waiting for them all to fall

### Allow unmasking

Find spaces — even small ones — where you can exist without performance. This might be alone time, time with trusted people who know about your ADHD, or environments where your natural behaviour is accepted.

### Address the basics

When in burnout, focus only on fundamentals: sleep, food, movement, hydration. Executive function recovery requires physical recovery first.

### Professional support

A therapist or coach who understands ADHD can help identify which aspects of your life are sustainable and which are burning you out. Medication adjustments may also be relevant.

### Rebuild slowly

Recovery from ADHD burnout isn't linear. It requires patience and the deliberate construction of a life that doesn't require constant masking to function. This might mean career changes, relationship renegotiation, or fundamental shifts in how you structure your days.

## The bigger picture

Masking exists because the world isn't designed for neurodivergent people. Every environment that demands masking is an environment that could be redesigned.

The goal isn't to teach people with ADHD to mask better. It's to build workplaces, relationships, and communities where masking isn't necessary — where different ways of thinking, moving, and being are simply part of the landscape.

Until that world exists, self-compassion and strategic unmasking are acts of survival.
