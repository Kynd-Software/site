# ADHD + Autism: The Overlap Nobody Talks About Enough

For a long time, you couldn't be diagnosed with both ADHD and autism. The DSM-IV explicitly prohibited dual diagnosis — a decision based on politics more than science. That changed in 2013, and since then, research has revealed what many people already knew from lived experience: these two conditions co-occur far more often than chance would predict.

## The numbers

Research suggests that 30–50% of autistic people also meet criteria for ADHD, and 20–50% of people with ADHD show significant autistic traits. The overlap is enormous — and it creates a presentation that doesn't neatly fit the textbook description of either condition alone.

## Why they were separated

The historical separation was based on surface-level behavioural observations:

- Autism = social withdrawal, rigid routines, intense focus
- ADHD = social impulsivity, chaotic routines, scattered focus

These looked like opposites. But when you look beneath the behaviours to the underlying neurology, the picture is more complex. Both conditions involve executive function differences, sensory processing variations, and difficulties with self-regulation — they just express differently.

## Where they overlap

### Executive function

Both ADHD and autism involve executive function differences, though often in different domains:

| | ADHD | Autism | Both |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Planning** | Difficulty starting | Difficulty with change | Difficulty with unstructured tasks |
| **Flexibility** | Chaotic shifting | Rigid resistance to shifting | Struggle with externally imposed change |
| **Working memory** | Poor | Variable | Information processing differences |
| **Self-monitoring** | Low self-awareness in the moment | Difficulty reading social context | Difficulty predicting how one is perceived |

### Sensory processing

Both conditions involve atypical sensory processing — but ADHD brains often seek stimulation (understimulated) while autistic brains are often overwhelmed by it (overstimulated). When both are present, the person may oscillate between seeking and avoiding sensory input, sometimes within the same day.

### Social differences

ADHD social difficulties tend toward impulsivity — interrupting, oversharing, missing turn-taking cues. Autistic social difficulties tend toward processing — difficulty reading subtext, missing unspoken rules, struggling with ambiguity. Combined, social situations become doubly taxing.

### Emotional intensity

Both conditions involve emotional regulation difficulties, though by different mechanisms. Combined, emotions can be both intense (ADHD) and difficult to identify or articulate (alexithymia, common in autism).

## Where they conflict

This is where the "AuDHD" experience gets particularly confusing:

**Routine vs. novelty.** The autistic brain craves predictability and routine. The ADHD brain craves novelty and stimulation. Living with both means simultaneously needing routine AND being bored by it. Needing change AND being distressed by it.

**Social drive vs. social exhaustion.** ADHD may create social impulsivity and desire for connection. Autism may create social exhaustion and need for solitude. The result: wanting to socialise but being drained by it, or avoiding socialising then feeling lonely.

**Hyperfocus vs. special interests.** Both involve intense concentration on specific things — but ADHD hyperfocus tends to shift between subjects, while autistic special interests tend to be sustained and deep. Combined, interests may be intensely pursued... then abruptly abandoned when the novelty fades, despite genuine love for the subject.

**Stimulation seeking vs. overwhelm.** Simultaneously needing more input (ADHD understimulation) and being overwhelmed by it (autistic sensory sensitivity). Finding the narrow band of "enough stimulation but not too much" becomes a daily negotiation.

## The diagnostic challenges

### Masking compounds

Both ADHD and autism involve masking — and combined, the masking can be extraordinarily effective but extraordinarily costly. The person may appear relatively neurotypical while internally managing two sets of differences simultaneously.

### Each masks the other

ADHD can mask autism: the impulsivity and social spontaneity of ADHD may look like typical social engagement, hiding underlying autistic social processing differences.

Autism can mask ADHD: the routines and systems built to manage autistic need for predictability may compensate for ADHD executive function difficulties, making both less visible.

### Diagnostic criteria assume "pure" presentations

Assessment tools for ADHD don't account for autistic traits, and vice versa. A clinician looking for "classic ADHD" may miss it because the autism modifies the presentation, and vice versa. This leads to:

- Getting only one diagnosis (whichever is more visible)
- Treatments that work for one condition but not the other
- Feeling that the diagnosis "doesn't quite fit"

## Living with both

### The energy equation

Managing two conditions simultaneously means higher baseline energy expenditure. Everything requires more processing — social situations, sensory environments, task management, emotional regulation. Burnout risk is significantly elevated.

### Self-understanding takes longer

With overlapping and contradictory needs, it takes longer to understand your own patterns. "Why did I enjoy that party but hate this one?" might require understanding both the ADHD need for stimulation AND the autistic threshold for sensory overload.

### Support needs are complex

Strategies that help ADHD (variety, novelty, spontaneity) can distress the autistic brain. Strategies that help autism (rigid routine, predictability, sameness) can bore the ADHD brain. The approach needs to be nuanced — finding strategies that serve both.

### Things that tend to help

- **Flexible routine** — consistent structure with built-in variation
- **Sensory management** — understanding your specific sensory profile and designing your environment accordingly
- **Self-compassion** — accepting that your needs are complex and sometimes contradictory
- **Energy budgeting** — recognising that your reserves deplete faster
- **Community** — connecting with others who share the dual experience
- **Both diagnoses** — having both recognised opens access to appropriate support for each

## Why this matters

The AuDHD experience is not simply "autism plus ADHD." The interaction between the two creates something distinct — a presentation with its own patterns, challenges, and strengths. Recognising this overlap helps:

- Clinicians provide more accurate assessment
- Individuals understand their own apparently contradictory needs
- Support strategies become appropriately nuanced
- The profound exhaustion of managing both is validated rather than dismissed

If you see yourself in both descriptions but "don't quite fit" either alone — you're not imagining it. The overlap is real, increasingly recognised, and deserving of support that acknowledges its full complexity.
