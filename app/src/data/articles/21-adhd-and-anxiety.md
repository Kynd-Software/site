# ADHD and Anxiety: Chicken, Egg, or Both?

If you have ADHD and anxiety, you're in good company. Research consistently shows that approximately 50% of adults with ADHD also meet criteria for an anxiety disorder. That's not a coincidence — and understanding the relationship between the two is essential for getting the right support.

But the relationship is complicated. ADHD can cause anxiety. Anxiety can mimic ADHD. And sometimes they're genuinely separate conditions that happen to coexist, each making the other worse.

## Three possible relationships

### 1. ADHD-generated anxiety

The most common pattern. Living with ADHD is objectively stressful. When you:

- Regularly forget important things
- Can't trust yourself to be on time
- Know you're capable but can't produce consistent results
- Face unpredictable executive function availability
- Carry years of negative feedback about your reliability

...anxiety is a logical response to an unpredictable internal environment.

This isn't an anxiety disorder in the traditional sense — it's a reasonable emotional response to living with unreliable executive functions. The anxiety is downstream of the ADHD. Treat the ADHD effectively, and this type of anxiety often reduces significantly.

**Common presentations:**
- Pre-event anxiety ("will I forget something? will I be late?")
- Performance anxiety ("can I do this today? what if my brain doesn't cooperate?")
- Social anxiety ("did I say something weird? did they notice I wasn't listening?")
- Generalised worry driven by realistic awareness of potential executive function failures

### 2. Anxiety mimicking ADHD

Anxiety itself can produce ADHD-like symptoms:

- Difficulty concentrating (mind occupied by worry)
- Restlessness and fidgeting (physical anxiety symptoms)
- Difficulty with memory (anxiety narrows cognitive bandwidth)
- Avoidance of tasks (fear-driven, not initiation-driven)
- Sleep difficulties (racing anxious thoughts vs. racing ADHD thoughts)

In some cases, what appears to be ADHD is actually anxiety — particularly in people who developed attention difficulties in adulthood rather than childhood. Careful clinical assessment can usually distinguish them, but misdiagnosis happens in both directions.

**Key difference:** ADHD attention difficulties are typically interest-based (you CAN focus on engaging things). Anxiety attention difficulties are typically pervasive (worry intrudes regardless of how interesting the task is).

### 3. True comorbidity

Sometimes ADHD and an independent anxiety disorder coexist. They have separate causes, separate mechanisms, and each makes the other worse:

- ADHD makes anxiety management harder (can't consistently do the meditation, the exercise, the routine)
- Anxiety makes ADHD harder (anxious arousal narrows focus even further, adds cognitive load)
- The combination creates a unique experience that neither diagnosis fully explains alone

## The overlap confusion

Several symptoms appear in both conditions:

| Symptom | In ADHD | In Anxiety |
|---|---|---|
| **Restlessness** | Understimulation seeking | Nervous system activation |
| **Poor concentration** | Interest-dependent | Worry-dependent |
| **Sleep difficulty** | Racing thoughts (varied content) | Racing thoughts (threat-focused) |
| **Avoidance** | Can't start (initiation) | Won't start (fear) |
| **Irritability** | Overstimulation/frustration | Overwhelm/threat response |
| **Forgetfulness** | Working memory deficit | Cognitive bandwidth limitation |

## Why getting the distinction right matters

### Medication implications

- ADHD stimulant medication can sometimes worsen anxiety (by increasing arousal in an already over-aroused system)
- But it can also *improve* anxiety (if the anxiety was ADHD-generated, treating ADHD reduces it)
- Anti-anxiety medication alone won't help ADHD symptoms
- The treatment sequence matters — and it depends on which pattern is present

### Therapy approaches

- CBT for anxiety focuses on challenging threat-based thinking
- CBT for ADHD focuses on building systems and self-compassion
- If anxiety is ADHD-generated, traditional anxiety CBT may not address the root cause
- If ADHD symptoms are anxiety-driven, ADHD coaching won't address the underlying fear

### Self-understanding

Knowing which pattern you're dealing with helps you:
- Stop blaming yourself for the wrong thing
- Seek the right type of support
- Understand why certain approaches haven't worked
- Communicate your needs accurately to clinicians and employers

## The compensatory anxiety pattern

One pattern deserves special attention: anxiety as an ADHD compensation strategy.

Many people with ADHD unconsciously use anxiety to compensate for executive function difficulties. The worry about forgetting something *keeps it in mind*. The fear of being late creates urgency that enables timely departure. The dread of a deadline generates the adrenaline needed to start.

This works — sort of. It gets things done. But it's deeply unpleasant, physiologically costly, and unsustainable. It's the equivalent of setting your house on fire to stay warm.

When ADHD is properly treated (medication, systems, support), this compensatory anxiety sometimes initially *increases* before it settles. The person is terrified of removing their only functioning system — even though that system was making them miserable.

## What helps

### If it's ADHD-generated anxiety:
- Prioritise ADHD treatment (medication, coaching, systems)
- Build external reliability (so you're not anxious about your own unreliability)
- Address the shame and self-blame that fuels the worry
- As ADHD symptoms improve, anxiety often naturally reduces

### If it's true comorbidity:
- Both conditions need separate treatment
- Work with a clinician experienced in both
- Start with whichever is more impairing (this varies by person)
- Be patient with medication adjustments — finding the right combination takes time

### For both:
- Regular exercise (one of the strongest evidence-based interventions for both)
- Sleep prioritisation (poor sleep worsens both dramatically)
- Nervous system regulation (breathing techniques, body-based practices)
- Community and connection (isolation worsens both)
- Self-compassion (anxiety AND ADHD both improve with reduced self-criticism)

## When to seek reassessment

Consider requesting reassessment if:
- You've been treated for anxiety for years but it hasn't improved
- Your attention difficulties began in childhood (suggests ADHD may be primary)
- Anti-anxiety medication helps your mood but not your functioning
- Your anxiety seems to be specifically about executive function failures
- Traditional anxiety treatments (relaxation, thought challenging) feel irrelevant to your experience

You deserve treatment that addresses what's actually happening — not just what it looks like from the outside.
